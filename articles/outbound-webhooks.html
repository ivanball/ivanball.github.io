    Sending Webhooks Without Opening a Door: Signed Delivery, Connect-Time SSRF Defense and a Lane of Its Own

    November 25, 2026 · Ivan Ball-llovera

    An outbound webhook is the one feature where an operator types a URL and your server dutifully connects to it, retries it for hours and signs everything it sends. Here is how MMCA.Common ships it: Standard Webhooks signatures with a rotation window, every delivery a durable internal command, an address policy enforced at the moment the socket opens, and a delivery lane that keeps a dead receiver from delaying anything else.

    A tenant admin pastes a URL into your settings page. "Notify us when a ticket opens." You save it, and the next event posts JSON to it.

    That URL is now operator input your server dials on its own schedule, and every obvious implementation of "post JSON to it" gets at least one of four things wrong.

    It trusts the URL. http://169.254.169.254/ is a perfectly valid absolute URI, and so is a hostname whose DNS answer is public when you validate it and private when you connect. Either way your server has just made a request into its own network on a stranger's behalf: server-side request forgery.

    It trusts the receiver. The receiver is down for the weekend, or answers in 29 seconds, and the loop that posts to it is the same loop that runs your business work, so a partner's outage becomes yours.

    It loses deliveries. A post from inside the request, or from a fire-and-forget task, dies with the process and leaves no record of what was owed.

    And it cannot prove anything. Without a signature the receiver cannot tell your post from anyone else's, and without a stable delivery id it cannot tell your retry from a second event.

    ADR-142 is the record of answering all four: stored subscriptions, HMAC-signed delivery scheduled as internal commands the framework already had, an outbound address policy enforced at validation time and again at connect time, and a dedicated delivery lane (ADR-142:35-37). It is the outbound twin of the inbound webhook edge in Article 49 (Undo Is a Feature: Saga Compensation and the Reconciliation Backstop), and it builds directly on the durable internal commands of Article 51 (Four Ways to Do Work Later: Channels, Cron, the Outbox and Durable Internal Commands), which this article assumes rather than re-teaches.

    The MMCA answer: three calls, and the rest is policy

    The whole feature is opt-in, and registering it turns nothing on by itself.

    AddWebhooks(configuration) (WebhookServiceCollectionExtensions.cs:67) binds the Webhooks section with data-annotation validation at startup (:71-74) and registers the subscription store, the administration service, the target validator, the signed sender, the secret protector, the DNS resolver and the event catalog (:76-82), plus data protection (:85), the hardened MMCA.Webhooks HTTP client (:87-102), a startup notice for an ignored development switch (:104) and the delivery lane (:106). Its own remarks spell out what else turning it on needs: pipeline.AddWebhookDelivery() inside AddMmcaApplicationPipeline, one AddWebhookEvent<TEvent>(name) per published event, the migration on the Webhooks:DataSourceName database, and a controller deriving from WebhookSubscriptionsControllerBase granted webhooks:manage (:33-39). With Webhooks:Enabled false, which is the default (WebhookSettings.cs:16-17), the fan-out stays inert, every administration endpoint answers 404 and no context maps the subscription table (WebhookServiceCollectionExtensions.cs:33-35).

    MMCA.Helpdesk is the one adopter, and its wiring is the shape:

    // MMCA.Helpdesk.Web/Program.cs (illustrative excerpt; the two calls are verbatim)
services.AddWebhooks(builder.Configuration);

builder.Services.AddMmcaApplicationPipeline(pipeline => pipeline
    // ... module scans ...
    .AddWebhookDelivery());   // the delivery handler, inside the pipeline so every decorator wraps it

// MMCA.Helpdesk.Tickets.Application/DependencyInjection.cs (verbatim)
services.AddWebhookEvent<TicketOpenedIntegrationEvent>("ticket.opened");

// appsettings.json: "Webhooks": { "Enabled": true }
// appsettings.Development.json: "Webhooks": { "AllowInsecureTargetsInDevelopment": true }

    One integration event, published under one webhook type name, to whatever subscriptions the tenant has created. That is the entire adopter surface. Said plainly, because it matters for how much weight to put on the rest of this article: Helpdesk is the only adopter, and the record states that nothing has been exercised against a real third-party receiver or a Standard Webhooks library on the receiving side; the evidence is the framework's unit and integration tiers (ADR-142:338-340).

    Fan-out: one event, one durable row per subscriber

    When the event arrives, WebhookFanOutHandler<TEvent> does four things in order (WebhookFanOutHandler.cs:60-123). It returns at once unless webhooks are enabled and the event type is registered in the catalog (:62-67). It lists the enabled subscriptions of the event's tenant and keeps the ones that subscribe to this event type (:69-78). It serializes the event once and skips the whole event with one warning when the payload is over MaxPayloadBytes (:80-86), default 65536, range 1024 to 1048576 (WebhookSettings.cs:51-56). And it schedules one DeliverWebhookInternalCommand per matching subscription, all inside one transaction (WebhookFanOutHandler.cs:88-115).

    The failure branch is the part worth copying. If any schedule fails, the handler throws (:117-123), and the exception message says why: "The event will be redelivered." A thrown handler is the outbox's (or the broker's) cue to redeliver the event, so a fan-out that could not record its rows gets another chance instead of silently delivering to half the subscribers.

    Each row carries a webhook-id computed by WebhookDeliveryId.For(eventId, subscriptionId): the first 16 bytes of SHA-256 over the event's MessageId followed by the subscription id, returned as a Guid (WebhookDeliveryId.cs:11-27). That determinism is what makes at-least-once delivery survivable on the other side. The outbox can redeliver an event, so the fan-out can run twice, but the same (event, subscription) pair always produces the same id (:5-8), and a receiver that dedupes on webhook-id sees one delivery. It is the receiver-side equivalent of the consumer inbox (ADR-021).

    From here a delivery is an ordinary internal command, so it inherits everything Article 51 describes: the durable row, the claim lease, the jittered backoff, the attempt ceiling, the dead letter and the operator console. Retries come from the InternalCommands:* settings, not from a webhook-specific policy (WebhookSettings.cs:8-9).

    Signing: Standard Webhooks, with a rotation window

    WebhookSignature implements the Standard Webhooks scheme (WebhookSignature.cs:8-13). A secret is whsec_ plus Base64 of 32 random bytes (:16, :28, GenerateSecret :32-33). A signature is v1, plus the Base64 HMAC-SHA256 over {id}.{timestamp}.{body} (Compute, :61-69), and the three values travel in the webhook-id, webhook-timestamp (Unix seconds) and webhook-signature headers (:18-25).

    The signature header is a space-separated list (:24-25), and that is what makes rotation painless. After a secret rotates, the previous secret keeps signing beside the new one for PreviousSecretGraceHours, default 24, range 0 to 168 (WebhookSettings.cs:58-60); the delivery handler asks the subscription for its signing secrets as of now (DeliverWebhookInternalCommandHandler.cs:64). A receiver that still holds the old secret keeps verifying during the window, and one that already holds the new secret verifies too.

    The framework also ships the receiver's half. WebhookSignature.Verify accepts a delivery when the timestamp lies within a tolerance of the receiver's clock and any v1, entry in the header matches, compared in constant time (:71-108, CryptographicOperations.FixedTimeEquals at :107). The tolerance check saturates at the long range, so a hostile timestamp cannot wrap the comparison or throw (:110-124). A malformed secret is a Webhooks.SecretUnreadable Result failure from DecodeSecret, never an exception (:38-53, :126-130).

    A receiver written against this contract looks like this:

    // Illustrative receiver; WebhookSignature.DecodeSecret and Verify are the real signatures.
var key = WebhookSignature.DecodeSecret(storedSecret);          // Result<byte[]>
if (key.IsFailure) return Results.StatusCode(500);

var ok = WebhookSignature.Verify(
    webhookId:       Request.Headers["webhook-id"],
    timestamp:       long.Parse(Request.Headers["webhook-timestamp"]),
    body:            rawBody,                                   // the exact bytes received, as text
    signatureHeader: Request.Headers["webhook-signature"],
    key:             key.Value,
    nowUnixSeconds:  DateTimeOffset.UtcNow.ToUnixTimeSeconds(),
    tolerance:       TimeSpan.FromMinutes(5));

if (!ok) return Results.Unauthorized();
// Dedupe on webhook-id before acting: the sender is at-least-once.

    The secrets are stored protected by the host's data-protection key ring (DataProtectionWebhookSecretProtector registered at WebhookServiceCollectionExtensions.cs:80, data protection at :85), which is why the registration remarks say a multi-replica host needs the shared key ring of ADR-069 to read a secret another replica wrote (:44-45). A replica that cannot read the secret does not throw: the outcome is SecretUnreadable, the row fails and retries (DeliverWebhookInternalCommandHandler.cs:113-120).

    SSRF: the check that counts is the one where the socket opens

    OutboundAddressPolicy is the rule. IsPublicAddress (OutboundAddressPolicy.cs:68) denies the private, loopback, link-local, shared, benchmarking, multicast and reserved IPv4 ranges plus the single cloud host endpoint 168.63.129.16 (:15), and judges an IPv4-mapped IPv6 address as its IPv4 form (:72). For IPv6 it is an allow-list: only global unicast 2000::/3 can be public (:35, :60), so a range nobody listed, including one added to the address architecture later, is refused by default. IsAcceptableTargetUri (:100) adds the URI rules: https only, and never localhost or *.localhost (:124-125).

    The obvious place to apply that rule is when the subscription is saved, and MMCA does: the registered target validator is DnsWebhookTargetValidator (WebhookServiceCollectionExtensions.cs:78), which the record describes as resolving the host at create and update and refusing when any answer is not public (ADR-142:132-135). But the record is explicit that this answer is early feedback only (ADR-142:135), because a validation-time check is defeated by DNS rebinding: the name resolves to a public address when you check it and to 10.0.0.5 when you connect.

    So the authoritative check lives in the HTTP client's ConnectCallback, wired into the primary SocketsHttpHandler (WebhookServiceCollectionExtensions.cs:95-99). WebhookConnectCallback resolves the host itself (WebhookConnectCallback.cs:37-40), refuses with WebhookTargetRefusedException before opening a socket when any resolved address is not public (:48-53), and then dials only the addresses it just checked, in order, returning the first that connects (:55-70). It never dials an address it did not check (:11-12), which is what makes the policy hold against DNS rebinding and against any handler earlier in the pipeline that rewrites the request URI (:12-14).

    One small judgement in that callback is worth noticing. An empty DNS answer is not a refusal: it throws a SocketException with HostNotFound, because an empty answer may be transient, so the delivery is retried (:42-46). A non-public answer, by contrast, is final. The delivery handler records a fixed reason ("Target refused: not a public address.") and completes the row, because retrying cannot change the answer, while the detail that can name the resolved address goes only to a server-side Warning, since a tenant admin API reads the last-failure column (DeliverWebhookInternalCommandHandler.cs:33-34, :92-98, :133-140). A stored target that is not https is refused by the sender before anything is sent, under its own fixed reason (:133-140).

    AllowInsecureTargetsInDevelopment lifts the scheme, localhost and private-address rules for local work, honoured only when the environment is Development and otherwise ignored with one startup warning (WebhookSettings.cs:69-73; the notice service at WebhookServiceCollectionExtensions.cs:104). Helpdesk sets it in its Development file only (appsettings.Development.json:10-11).

    A client that does exactly one thing

    The MMCA.Webhooks client's primary handler sets AllowAutoRedirect = false, UseProxy = false, UseCookies = false and ActivityHeadersPropagator = null (WebhookServiceCollectionExtensions.cs:89-94), then clears every additional handler a host default adds and removes the client's default loggers (:101-102). Each of those is a deliberate refusal, and the remarks give the reasons (:47-55):

    • No redirect. A 3xx is not followed; the handler treats it as a failed status that retries (DeliverWebhookInternalCommandHandler.cs:113-114), so the only address ever dialled is the stored one.
    • No second retry policy. A host-wide resilience pipeline would retry beside the internal-command queue; clearing the handlers leaves the queue as the only retry and the only failure policy.
    • No URL in the logs. The target URL often carries the receiver's token in its path, and the client loggers would write it.
    • No trace context to a third party. No traceparent, tracestate or baggage header leaves the host.

    The request timeout is TimeoutSeconds (:87-88), default 10, range 1 to 30 (WebhookSettings.cs:25-34).

    The outcome mapping is short and every branch is a decision (DeliverWebhookInternalCommandHandler.cs:79-125). A 2xx completes the row (:85-90). A 410 Gone records the failure, disables the subscription and completes the row (:101-111). A refused target completes the row (:92-98). Anything else (any other status, redirects included, a transport failure, timeouts included, or an unreadable secret) fails the row so the processor retries with backoff and dead-letters after MaxAttempts (:113-124). A missing, deleted or disabled subscription completes the row without sending (:52-57), and a host where Webhooks:Enabled is false fails the row before reading anything, so a row queued before webhooks were switched off retries, dead-letters and stays requeue-able (:41-50). The handler is deliberately not ITransactional: the outcome is recorded on its own save (:12-16, :69).

    The lane: a dead receiver cannot delay anything else

    Here is the problem the first five sections do not solve. Internal commands are a shared queue. Business commands run on it too. A receiver that takes 10 seconds to time out, multiplied by a backlog of its deliveries, is 10 seconds of every cycle your business commands do not run.

    So delivery rows get a lane of their own. WebhookDeliveryLane routes them to their own InternalCommandLaneProcessor, never the shared loop, so a slow or failing receiver cannot delay the other internal commands (WebhookDeliveryLane.cs:14-19). It registers the lane only when both Webhooks:Enabled and InternalCommands:Enabled are true (:51-67). The lane is named webhooks, the value of its lane metric tag (:22-23), and it is built from three settings (:36-49): the cooldown is ReceiverCooldownSeconds (:43), the concurrency is DeliveryConcurrency (:47), default 4, range 1 to 16 (WebhookSettings.cs:36-41), and the batch start budget is TimeoutSeconds (:48). The receiver key is the subscription id (:44-46).

    Inside a claimed batch up to that many rows run at once per replica, two rows of one receiver never run at the same time, and the earliest allowed row starts first (InternalCommandLaneProcessor.cs:28-30). No row starts once the start budget has elapsed since the batch's first start, and the rows that never started are released uncharged, so they are due again at once (:31-33, ReleaseAsync :542-558).

    The cooldown charges an attempt without sending

    This is the part of the design that is easiest to get wrong, so it is worth reading slowly.

    A receiver whose real attempt failed cools down on this replica (:36-37). While it cools down, each of its rows whose turn comes is not sent and its handler is not invoked; it is charged one failed attempt through the unchanged failure path, with the error code InternalCommands.ReceiverCoolingDown and the message "Receiver cooling down after a failed delivery." (:37-39, :60-64, ChargeAsync :519-540). The first row whose turn comes after the cooldown ends runs for real; a success closes the cooldown, a failure reopens it (:39-41, ApplyOutcome :502-517). A cooldown opens from the instant the attempt failed, not from when the scheduler got round to it (:495-500, OpenCooldown :564-576).

    Why charge? Because the alternative, deferring a cooling receiver's rows for free, lets a permanently dead receiver cost one real attempt per cooldown per replica forever, and a subscription receiving events faster than that accumulates pending rows without bound (ADR-142:297-304). Charging keeps each row on the normal retry timeline, so a dead receiver's backlog dead-letters on the normal attempt budget.

    The obvious objection is that charging could dead-letter a row without ever really trying it. The clamp answers that. ReceiverCooldownSeconds (default 60, range 5 to 120, WebhookSettings.cs:43-49) is not applied as configured: the effective cooldown is min(configured, S - attemptTimeout - 1 s), never below 1 second, where S is the shortest possible span from a row's first attempt to its last under the queue's backoff settings, and the cooldown is off when S is zero (InternalCommandLaneProcessor.cs:89-116). Leaving room for one attempt in flight is what guarantees, on any number of replicas, that a receiver answering from the moment a row is scheduled gets at least one real attempt on that row before it can dead-letter, unless the 1-second floor applied (:93-96).

    Trade-offs, honestly

    • It has not met a real receiver. Helpdesk is the only adopter (Program.cs:107, :160; DependencyInjection.cs:60), and nothing has been exercised against a real third-party receiver or a Standard Webhooks library on the receiving side (ADR-142:338-340). The signature format follows the scheme as the source documents it; interoperability is asserted, not observed.
    • The whole event is the payload. There is no projection step: the fan-out serializes the event as it is (WebhookFanOutHandler.cs:80), so registering an event publishes every member of its contract to a third party. Register only terminal contracts you would be comfortable printing.
    • The cooldown is per replica. It is held on each replica (WebhookSettings.cs:44-46), so R replicas each spend a real attempt per window on a failing receiver.
    • Recovery after an outage costs a window. Because cooling rows are charged, a row whose receiver comes back too close to the row's last attempt can dead-letter without another real try. That is the stated price of the charged fail-fast (ADR-142:264-266), and the dead letter is requeue-able from the internal-command console.
    • Only 410 disables a subscription (DeliverWebhookInternalCommandHandler.cs:106-111). A receiver that fails forever with a 500 keeps being retried and dead-lettered event by event, bounded by the charged cooldown rather than by a circuit that turns the subscription off.
    • A slow but succeeding receiver never cools down. A success closes the cooldown (InternalCommandLaneProcessor.cs:513-516), and one receiver never runs two rows at once (:28-30), so its backlog drains at one row per response time per replica.
    • Many distinct failing receivers still fill the lane. A hanging receiver holds a slot for up to TimeoutSeconds, so DeliveryConcurrency or more of them can occupy every slot; one tenant can hold up to MaxSubscriptionsPerTenant subscriptions (default 25, a soft limit, WebhookSettings.cs:62-67). One tenant can delay other tenants' deliveries. It cannot delay the shared queue.
    • Every replica needs the same Webhooks:Enabled. A replica that drains the queue with webhooks off registers no lane (WebhookDeliveryLane.cs:64-67), and its handler fails delivery rows without sending (DeliverWebhookInternalCommandHandler.cs:41-50), spending attempts toward the dead letter.
    • The client trace still records the URL path. Loggers and trace headers are removed, but the HTTP client span is not, so a host exporting traces to a shared backend filters the MMCA.Webhooks spans itself (WebhookServiceCollectionExtensions.cs:53-54).

    Apply this even without MMCA

    The design ports to any stack with a relational database and a background worker. Six steps:

    1. Make each delivery a durable row, written in one transaction per event. One row per (event, subscriber), all or nothing, and if the write fails, fail the event handler so your event transport redelivers it. A post from inside the request is a delivery you cannot prove you owed.
    2. Derive the delivery id from the event and the subscriber. A hash of the pair, not a fresh GUID, so a redelivered event produces the same id and the receiver can dedupe. At-least-once is only tolerable when the duplicate is recognizable.
    3. Sign with a public scheme and let the signature header carry a list. Standard Webhooks gives your receivers a library instead of your documentation. A list header makes secret rotation a grace window instead of a coordinated cutover, and ship a constant-time verifier with a timestamp tolerance.
    4. Check the address where the socket opens, and dial only what you checked. Resolve the name yourself in the connect callback, refuse if any answer is non-public, then connect to those addresses and no others. Treat IPv6 as an allow-list. Treat an empty answer as transient and a private answer as final. Validation at save time is a courtesy to the admin, not a control.
    5. Strip the client to one handler. No redirects, no proxy, no cookies, no trace propagation, no URL-logging handler, no second retry policy. Every default you leave on is a channel you did not decide to open.
    6. Give deliveries their own lane, and charge cooling receivers. Isolate the drain from your business work, key concurrency by receiver, and when a receiver is failing, fail its due rows on the normal retry timeline without sending, clamped so every row still gets one real attempt before it can dead-letter.

    The rule of thumb: an outbound webhook is a URL a stranger chose, dialled by your server on your schedule. Decide where it may connect at the socket, not at the form, and decide what its outage may cost you before it has one.

    What we covered: why outbound webhooks fail in four distinct ways (SSRF, a slow receiver, lost work and unprovable posts), how AddWebhooks registers an inert feature that two further calls (AddWebhookDelivery and one AddWebhookEvent per event), a migration, an admin controller and a flag turn on, how the fan-out schedules one durable internal command per subscriber in one transaction and throws to get the event redelivered, why the deterministic webhook-id makes at-least-once dedupable, how Standard Webhooks signatures and a space-separated header give secret rotation a grace window, why the authoritative SSRF check is a connect callback that dials only the addresses it checked, what the stripped MMCA.Webhooks client refuses and why, how each send outcome maps onto the row, and how the delivery lane, its per-receiver concurrency and its charged, clamped cooldown keep a dead receiver from delaying anything else.
